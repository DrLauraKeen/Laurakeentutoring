The Odyssey Effect: Why Classics Departments Are Buzzing

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey has done in one summer what many of us spend careers trying to do — made Homer feel urgent again.

This August, Classics News is given over entirely to a film. Christopher Nolan's adaptation of The Odyssey — starring Matt Damon as Odysseus, Tom Holland as Telemachus and Anne Hathaway as Penelope, shot on IMAX film and reportedly his most expensive production to date — opened in cinemas on 17 July, and it has done something I did not expect a Hollywood blockbuster to do: it has made staff-room conversations in Classics departments genuinely excited again.

The trailer

The official trailer for The Odyssey (Universal Pictures, dir. Christopher Nolan)

A field usually starved of headlines, suddenly everywhere

What's striking isn't just that a film about Homer exists — adaptations come and go — but the scale of the academic response building around it, much of it close to home. Cambridge's Faculty of Classics has been publishing a running commentary on the film: Professors Richard Hunter and Rebecca Laemmle discussing what Nolan does with the poem's underworld scene, and Professor Mary Beard, writing in The Guardian and The Times, weighing what the film keeps of Homer's wit and what it flattens. The Guardian has even run a beginner's guide to tackling the original text for anyone who leaves the cinema wanting more.

It isn't only happening in lecture halls. Regional branches of the Classical Association have been running Homer-themed book clubs and discussing group screenings, reporting that the film has already brought new faces to events that might otherwise pass unnoticed outside the subject's usual audience — while museums have built entire public programmes around the release.

Every so often, popular culture hands Classics teachers a gift: a reason for a student to walk into the room already curious. Whatever you make of Nolan's choices, that's worth taking seriously.
— DR LAURA KEEN

Using it in lessons, not just talking about it

For teaching, the film's real value isn't as a substitute for the text but as a way in. Several university lecturers interviewed in the run-up to release described a straightforward approach that works just as well at GCSE and A Level: watch, then compare. Where does Nolan follow Homer closely? Where does he depart, and why might a director in 2026 make that choice? Those are exactly the questions that turn a set text into an argument worth having — and they travel well whether the student has read Robert Fagles, Emily Wilson, or is meeting Odysseus for the very first time.

If this is the term you finally sit down with the Odyssey in the original Greek, or want to bring some of that classroom energy into a GCSE or A Level Classical Civilisation module, I'd love to help.

Reading the Unreadable: AI Gives Us Back a Lost Greek Voice

Buried by Vesuvius in AD 79 and unread ever since — a scorched papyrus has just given up thirty new lines of Greek philosophy, and machine learning is quietly rewriting how much of the ancient world we'll ever get to read.

It's easy to assume the classical corpus is a fixed, finished thing — the texts we have are the texts there are. This spring's news from Cairo is a good reminder that isn't quite true. Researchers examining a papyrus fragment identified thirty previously unpublished verses by the Presocratic philosopher Empedocles, a genuinely rare addition to a body of work that survives mostly in tantalising fragments quoted by later authors.

A find that reshapes, not just adds

Empedocles is one of those figures every A Level Classical Civilisation student meets briefly and wishes they had more of: the philosopher who proposed that all matter is built from four eternal roots — earth, water, air and fire — locked in cycles of Love and Strife. Because so little of his poetry survives complete, every new line matters disproportionately. Scholars now have to weigh not just what these verses say, but how they might shift existing readings of the philosophy built around the fragments we already had.

A Greek papyrus fragment carrying lines from Homer's Odyssey, ca. 285–250 BCE — the kind of everyday survival that projects like the Vesuvius Challenge are trying to multiply. (The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Public Domain)

The bigger story: scrolls nobody could open

The Empedocles papyrus sits alongside an even more remarkable ongoing project: the Vesuvius Challenge, which uses X-ray scanning and machine learning to "virtually unroll" the carbonised scrolls from Herculaneum — scrolls so fragile that physically opening them destroys them. What began in 2023 as a handful of legible Greek words from one scroll has, over the past year, become a serious push to read entire scrolls in full, with hundreds more still waiting in storage.

Why it's worth mentioning in lessons

For students, this is a genuinely useful corrective to the idea that Classics is a fixed syllabus of texts settled centuries ago. It's also a nice way into talking about transmission — how fragile and accidental our access to the ancient world really is, and how much of what we call "the classics" survives by luck, later quotation, or, increasingly, twenty-first-century computer vision.

Marble, Mud and Coin Hoards: A Season of Digs

From a bilingual price list carved into a Roman basilica to 200,000 animal bones telling the story of a Greek feast, early summer brought a run of finds that put real evidence behind the texts we teach.

Term-time doesn't leave much room for archaeology news to filter into lessons, so this post is a round-up of the finds from earlier in the year that are worth returning to — each one a small, concrete way of making the ancient world feel less like a fixed text and more like an ongoing conversation with the evidence.

A price list in two languages

At Aphrodisias in modern Turkey, archaeologists have spent over two decades piecing together 275 marble fragments of the Prices Edict — the Emperor Diocletian's attempt to cap runaway inflation across the Roman Empire. What makes the inscription such a good teaching example is its bilingualism: the text runs in both Latin and Greek, a practical acknowledgement that most literate residents of the city could read Greek but not Latin. It's a small, vivid piece of evidence for how Rome's empire actually worked on the ground, language politics included.

Marble ruins at Ephesus, Turkey — the same landscape of Greco-Roman inland Anatolia that Aphrodisias belongs to.

Feasting, coins, and a curse

Elsewhere, a study of roughly 200,000 animal bones has reconstructed the scale and rhythm of public feasting in ancient Greece, while separate research into Roman coin hoards examined why so many caches were buried and simply never reclaimed — a quietly poignant detail about disrupted lives. A newly published Roman-era curse tablet, meanwhile, mixed Latin content with Greek lettering and invoked Egyptian deities against four named individuals, a reminder of how thoroughly these cultures overlapped in daily life, magic included.

Bringing it into the classroom

None of these finds need a full lesson built around them, but they're excellent five-minute hooks: a bilingual inscription for a discussion of language and empire, a curse tablet for anyone squeamish about "boring" epigraphy, or a coin hoard for a conversation about what the absence of evidence can tell us too.
